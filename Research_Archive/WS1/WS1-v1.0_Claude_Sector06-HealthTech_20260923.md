# Sector 6 — HealthTech

## Part 1 — Market structure and size

- **No reliable data identified** on HealthTech sector size, revenue or enterprise count in Sierra Leone — consistent with the existing draft.

- **A sharp, quantified capacity gap not in the existing draft:** the density of doctors, nurses and midwives in Sierra Leone is estimated at **6.4 per 10,000 population**, against a WHO-recommended minimum threshold of **44.5 per 10,000** needed for meaningful progress toward universal health coverage — roughly a **seven-fold shortfall**. [Annals of Global Health / PMC](https://annalsofglobalhealth.org/articles/10.5334/aogh.5338) — **†, not opened**. This is one of the starkest quantified capacity gaps found anywhere across the sectors assessed so far.

- **Financing and coverage baseline:** **less than 1% of Sierra Leone's population is covered by any social health protection scheme.** Out-of-pocket health expenditure stands at **46%** of total health spending, and the government's own UHC service coverage index score is **41** (out of 100). [WHO / Ministry of Health and Sanitation](https://mohs.gov.sl/universal-health-coverage/) — **†, not opened**.

## Part 2 — The structural problem

- **Core problem confirmed, now with a specific, decade-long institutional failure identified as its cause.** The **Sierra Leone Social Health Insurance Scheme (SLeSHI)** — the country's would-be national health-insurance rail — has been under discussion since 2007 and was formally launched in 2017, but **the scheme itself never became operational.** [WHO/AFRO](https://www.afro.who.int/countries/sierra-leone/news/sierra-leone-moves-closer-universal-health-coverage-high-level-engagement-draft-slauhc-bill) — **†, not opened**. This directly explains why no digital-health payer or insurance rail exists for a HealthTech company to build on — it is not merely that inclusion is low, it is that the specific mechanism designed to fix it has sat unused for close to a decade.

- **A second, deep structural problem not in the existing draft: the public health workforce is substantially unpaid.** A mixed-methods survey across 10 of Sierra Leone's 16 districts (2023–2024) found that **50.6% of clinical staff at primary health units were unsalaried**, operating outside formal payroll systems with limited accountability. Separately, **approximately 10,000 auxiliary nurses have been engaged in the public health system since 2010 on an unsalaried "volunteer" basis.** [PMC, "Improving Sierra Leone's skilled health-worker-to-population ratio"](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12636922/) — **†, not opened**. This matters directly for HealthTech investability: the "formal" health system any digital-health company would serve is materially smaller, and less financially stable, than headline facility or workforce counts suggest.

- **Where value/capacity concentrates:** with no functioning domestic insurance rail, financing and delivery capacity has flowed almost entirely through donor and NGO vertical programmes (Gavi, Global Fund, PEPFAR, USAID, UNICEF — see Part 7) rather than through a commercial or even fully functioning public system — this is a more specific answer than the existing draft's general "donor-dependent" framing.

## Part 3 — Is this sector investable now?

- **Still no reliable evidence of a revenue-generating Sierra Leonean HealthTech firm — confirmed, not overturned, in this pass.** **SalCare**, already logged in the existing draft as pilot-stage, is confirmed as a mobile micro-health wallet and insurance app that lets families save small amounts, buy affordable micro-insurance, and pay for care from basic phones, co-designed with residents to reach women, youth and people with disabilities specifically. [App Store listing / related coverage] — **†, not opened**. **No user count or revenue data was identified in this pass either** — it remains genuinely pilot-stage, not merely under-documented.

- **mPharma (Ghana) — the region's flagship pharmaceutical-supply-chain HealthTech company — is confirmed not operating in Sierra Leone, with fresh 2024–2025 sourcing.** mPharma has raised **over US$40 million cumulatively**, including a **US$13.6 million round involving Sanofi's Global Health Unit Impact Fund**, and has taken strategic investment from **Growth Investment Partners (GIP) Ghana**, a platform established by British International Investment (BII); as of late 2025 it was doubling down on **Francophone Africa expansion**. It operates in **Ghana, Nigeria, Kenya and Zambia** — Sierra Leone is not among them, now or in its stated expansion pipeline. [Launchbase Africa](https://launchbaseafrica.com/2024/10/14/ghanaian-healthtech-mpharma-doubles-down-on-profitable-francophone-africa-expansion/) — **†, not opened**.

- **Reliance Health (Nigeria)** raised **US$40 million in a Series B round led by General Atlantic (2022)** — the largest Series B by an African health-tech startup to date. This is what genuine venture-scale investability looks like in this sector regionally; nothing remotely comparable exists in or near Sierra Leone.

- **A genuine, Sierra-Leone-specific innovation exists, but it is infrastructure, not a company — worth distinguishing clearly from the existing draft's undifferentiated "not investable."** **HETA (Health Electrification and Telecommunications Alliance)**, Power Africa's flagship health-facility electrification and connectivity initiative, builds on **HealthGrid Sierra Leone** — a USAID-supported Global Development Alliance with Orange Sierra Leone, World Vision Sierra Leone, Gavi and the Ministry of Health and Sanitation, which installed solar power and internet at **26–27 remote health facilities** (pilot completed between September 2024 and January 2025 depending on source — **minor date discrepancy, logged, not resolved**) — and is now expanding to **an additional 60 sites.** [RESOLVE](https://www.resolve.ngo/news/resolves-healthgrid-sierra-leone-successfully-concludes-paving-way-for-heta-sierra-leone-and-a-pioneering-new-sustainability-mechanism); [Abt Global](https://www.abtglobal.com/projects/heta-powering-health-with-clean-energy-in-africa) — both **†, not opened**. A named commercial solar-energy company, **Mopo**, partners with HETA to deliver this power. This is not a HealthTech company an investor could back, but it is a real precondition — reliable power and connectivity at the facility level — for any future digital-health venture to operate at all, and should be logged as such rather than folded into a blanket "nothing here" finding.

- **Minimum viable business model:** unchanged from the existing draft's conclusion, now with the specific mechanism identified — with SLeSHI non-operational and out-of-pocket expenditure at 46%, there is no functioning payer rail for a digital-health company to plug into today.

- **Venture-scale potential:** **no**, confirmed, and now more sharply explained: the formal system any HealthTech company would serve is both smaller (given the workforce/payroll informality finding) and less financially stable (given the aid-cuts finding in Part 7) than headline figures suggest.

## Part 4 — Capital and instrument fit

- **What is needed:** overwhelmingly grant and donor/blended infrastructure finance (as HETA/HealthGrid demonstrates), not equity. Any future digital-health equity company would first need either a functioning payer rail or a viable out-of-pocket/B2B revenue model — neither currently exists at scale.

- **Ticket sizes:** **no reliable Sierra Leone-specific figure identified.** Regional comparators, for context only: mPharma's cumulative >$40m raised (including the $13.6m Sanofi-linked round) and Reliance Health's $40m Series B (General Atlantic) illustrate that venture-scale outcomes in this sector require markets an order of magnitude larger, more urbanised, and better-insured than Sierra Leone offers today.

- **A useful regional funding-pattern signal (proxy, not Sierra Leone-specific):** across Africa, pharmaceutical-supply-chain/logistics/embedded-financing HealthTech models (mPharma-like) capture roughly **40% of total sector VC funding**, while consumer telemedicine platforms (Reliance Health-like) capture only around **17%.** [via search, source not independently opened] — **†**. This suggests that *if* Sierra Leone ever attracts HealthTech capital, a pharmaceutical-supply-chain/logistics entry point — building on the existing donor-run RapidPro medicines-tracking and NPPU procurement infrastructure (Part 7) — is more plausible than a consumer telemedicine app, given the near-total absence of a private-pay or insured customer base.

- **Financing gap:** still **no reliable Sierra Leone-specific figure identified.**

## Part 5 — Employment and job readiness

- **Skills base, now quantified:** the 6.4-per-10,000 health-worker density against a WHO minimum of 44.5-per-10,000 (Part 1) is a direct, sourced measure of talent constraint, replacing the existing draft's unsupported general statement.

- **Gender — a new, sourced figure replacing the existing draft's "no reliable data identified":** **64.2% of Sierra Leone's active health workforce are female.** [Search-sourced, PMC-linked reporting] — **†, not opened**.

- **A major structural employment finding not in the existing draft:** roughly half of primary-health-unit clinical staff (50.6%, 2023–24 survey) and ~10,000 auxiliary nurses since 2010 are **unsalaried** — meaning a large share of this sector's "employment" is unpaid labour outside the formal payroll and accountability system, not jobs a private investment would create or capture value from. This should materially qualify any future employment-multiplier claim made for this sector.

- **Youth employment potential:** **no reliable data identified** specific to this sector, consistent with the existing draft.

## Part 6 — Investment innovation and de-risking

- **This pass corrects the existing draft's blanket "no mechanisms evidenced" position for this sector.** **HETA's "Sustainability Fund"** is a real, named, Sierra-Leone-specific innovation: rather than treating solar/connectivity electrification of health facilities as a one-off donor grant, HETA explicitly incorporates market-based revenue-generation mechanisms intended to fund ongoing operations and maintenance after the initial donor-funded build — an attempt to convert a grant-funded infrastructure intervention into something closer to a self-sustaining, quasi-commercial model. [Abt Global; SEforALL](https://www.seforall.org/news/momentum-is-growing-for-powering-healthcare) — **†, both not opened**. This is genuinely closer to "investment innovation" than anything found in this sector previously, even though it operates at the infrastructure layer rather than the company layer.

- **Other evidenced TA/donor mechanisms:** UNICEF's **RapidPro** SMS-based medicines-tracking system (USAID-supported, since 2015) provides free-to-user, basic-phone-compatible drug-distribution feedback — a real, functioning digital tool, though donor-run rather than a company; **Crown Agents** was contracted in 2012 to build and manage the **National Pharmaceutical Procurement Unit (NPPU)** and transfer local capacity over three years — a foreign-contracted national logistics function, not a Sierra Leonean venture, but evidence that a functioning pharma-supply-chain backbone has been built once before with external management.

- **What would need to be true for commercial entry without concessional support:** SLeSHI or its proposed SLAUHC successor (Part 7) actually becoming operational as a functioning payer; resolved workforce formalisation so a digital tool has a stable, trackable professional user base; and, per Part 4's funding-pattern signal, a pharma-logistics entry point building on existing donor-run infrastructure (RapidPro, NPPU) is more plausible than a consumer-facing app.

## Part 7 — Enabling environment

- **New, directly relevant policy development not in the existing draft:** a draft **Sierra Leone Agency for Universal Health Coverage (SLAUHC) Bill** proposes creating a single agency to integrate the country's two major health-financing mechanisms — the **Free Healthcare Initiative (FHCI)**, in place since 2010 for pregnant/lactating women and under-5s, and the never-operationalised **SLeSHI** (Part 2) — under one governance structure. A high-level stakeholder engagement (60+ senior leaders) was held **6 May 2025**; **no reliable data identified confirming the Bill has since passed.** [WHO/AFRO](https://www.afro.who.int/countries/sierra-leone/news/sierra-leone-moves-closer-universal-health-coverage-high-level-engagement-draft-slauhc-bill) — **†, not opened**. The government's own 2025–2030 UHC targets — raising the service coverage index from 41 to 55, cutting out-of-pocket expenditure from 46% to below 20%, and **"mobilising significant private sector investment"** — are directly on-point for WS1's investability question and were entirely absent from the existing draft.

- **A major negative shock, not in the existing draft, that materially worsens this sector's financing picture:** at the end of January 2025, the US administration issued a **stop-work order** halting USAID-funded projects. Sierra Leone's Ministry of Health reported this represents a **US$45 million reduction** in projects focused on maternal, child and adolescent health; the US had provided **US$3.2 million in 2024 — about 14% of Sierra Leone's total reproductive-health funding** — now cut. Separately, the UK's "Saving Lives" programme was reduced from roughly **£17 million (~US$22.9m) to just £1 million (~US$1.3m)** by late 2025. [Human Rights Watch-linked reporting via Fuller Project, Foreign Policy, Rebuild Consortium] — **†, all not opened**. Reported consequences include a sharp decline in per-capita health expenditure and specific emerging service gaps in rural, donor-dependent districts — **Pujehun, Bonthe, Kambia, Karene and Falaba are named specifically.** This is a large, dated, quantified funding contraction directly affecting the sector's dominant financing model (donor/NGO delivery, per Part 2) and should be weighted heavily in any current investability judgement — the existing draft's single line ("the 2025 reduction in US health aid removed part of it") substantially understates the scale of what has happened.

- **Donor/development programme ecosystem, now more fully mapped:** USAID/Power Africa (HETA/HealthGrid), Gavi, the Global Fund (which procures ARVs jointly with or exclusively for Sierra Leone), PEPFAR, the US President's Malaria Initiative, UNICEF (RapidPro), and Crown Agents (NPPU) — a dense donor ecosystem that is now visibly contracting per the aid-cuts finding above, not a stable baseline to plan against.

- **Infrastructure constraints:** HETA/HealthGrid's own existence — donor intervention was required simply to bring solar power and internet to health facilities — is itself direct evidence of the same power/connectivity constraint logged cross-sector, now with a concrete remediation programme and site count attached.

## Part 8 — Comparative evidence

- **Ghana (mPharma) and Nigeria (Reliance Health) remain the comparators, now with hard, dated funding figures rather than general statements.** Both operate in markets with materially larger populations, higher urbanisation, and — in mPharma's case — an actual functioning pharmacy-distribution network to build a supply-chain business on top of; Sierra Leone has neither the population scale nor (per Part 2) a functioning insurance rail to offer an equivalent foundation today.

- **What failed, reframed for this sector specifically:** no Sierra Leone-specific HealthTech venture has failed outright — there has essentially been nothing at venture scale to fail. The closest thing to a "failure" evidenced in this pass is a **funding-model failure**: the sector's near-total dependence on donor/NGO delivery has proven fragile, as the 2025 US and UK aid cuts (Part 7) directly demonstrate. This is arguably a more important comparative lesson than any single-company failure would be.

> **Verdict.** **Not investable now — confirmed, with a materially sharper and more current basis than the existing draft.** This is not a static absence of activity; it is an active double pressure: on the supply side, a formal health workforce that is both critically undersized (6.4 vs. a WHO minimum of 44.5 per 10,000) and roughly half unsalaried; on the financing side, a donor-dependent delivery model now undergoing a large, sudden, dated contraction (US and UK aid cuts through 2025). **What would have to change:** SLeSHI or its proposed SLAUHC successor actually becoming operational as a functioning payer rail; workforce formalisation; and — the live, unresolved question right now — whether the sector's donor-funded infrastructure (HETA's 2024–25 electrification work, RapidPro, NPPU) can survive and adapt to the 2025 aid contraction, or whether it simply loses capacity. Stage 2 should treat this last question as urgent and time-sensitive, not a standing background fact.
