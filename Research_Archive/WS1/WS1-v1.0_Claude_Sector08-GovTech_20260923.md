# Sector 8 — GovTech

## Part 1 — Market structure and size

- **Confirmed: a public-sector function, not a private market** — consistent with the existing draft, now much more specifically documented. Anchor institutions: the **Directorate of Science, Technology and Innovation (DSTI)**, housed in the Office of the President and led by **Dr David Sengeh, Sierra Leone's first Chief Innovation Officer**, and the **Ministry of Communication, Technology and Innovation (MoCTI)**. Named platforms and projects include **WanGov** (the national citizen-facing digital government services portal), a **tax portal** (tax laws, calculations, calendar), a **land registry portal** (digitising land records and titles), and **EPETS-1** (a partnership with the Ministry of Finance to move ministries from manual to digital annual budget submission). [DSTI](https://dsti.gov.sl/); [civictech.guide](https://directory.civictech.guide/listing/directorate-of-science-technology-and-innovation-dsti-sierra-leone) — **†, both not opened**.

- **A large, previously unlogged capital figure:** the **Sierra Leone Digital Transformation Project (SLDTP)** is a flagship government initiative funded by a **US$50 million grant from the World Bank's IDA**, targeting broadband access expansion, digital-skills development and government capacity to deliver services digitally. [projects.worldbank.org](https://projects.worldbank.org/en/projects-operations/project-detail/P177077) — **†, not opened**. This is the largest, most concrete capital figure identified anywhere in this sector, and was not in the existing draft at all.

- **Core public financial management (PFM) systems, named and quantified:** the **Integrated Financial Management Information System (IFMIS)**, rolled out progressively since 2013, has expanded from a baseline of **12 MDAs to 61 MDAs**; the Bank of Sierra Leone's **T24** payment system; the **ASYCUDA World** customs system; and the National Revenue Authority's **Integrated Tax Administration System (ITAS)**. [World Bank PFM documentation] — **†, not opened**. **Note a nuance against the Sector 5 (Logistics/trade-tech) finding**, which reported Sierra Leone's customs delegation visiting Liberia in August 2025 specifically to learn from Liberia's more advanced ASYCUDA World implementation — this suggests Sierra Leone has *some* ASYCUDA deployment but lags on its fuller capability; **flagged for Stage 2 reconciliation, not treated as a contradiction.**

## Part 2 — The structural problem

- **Core problem confirmed (manual, opaque public processes; public-finance leakage), now illustrated by the single best-evidenced digitisation case study found across the entire eight-sector assessment.** In **December 2014**, Sierra Leone switched payment of Ebola response workers from cash to mobile money — **from 100% cash to 100% mobile money within one payment cycle**, covering roughly **13,000–16,000 workers.** Before digitisation, cash payments were **"slow, inaccurate, and open to graft and theft,"** took **over a month**, and had caused large-scale worker strikes. After digitisation, payment time fell to **about a week**, saving **US$10.7 million** for government, taxpayers, development partners and response workers combined — an amount the source describes as **equivalent to funding Sierra Leone's Free Health Care Programme for 1.4 million children and 250,000 pregnant women for a year** — plus roughly **US$80,000 per month** in avoided worker travel costs and the recovery of **around 800 working days per month.** This is corroborated across multiple independent sources: a UN/Better Than Cash Alliance case study, UNCDF, and ReliefWeb reporting. [Better Than Cash Alliance](https://www.betterthancash.org/alliance-reports/saving-money-saving-lives-a-case-study-on-the-benefits-of-digitizing-payments-to-ebola-response-workers-in-sierra-leone); [UNCDF](https://www.uncdf.org/article/1474/digitizing-payments-to-ebola-response-workers-migration) — **†, both blocked, not opened, but the figures are consistent across at least four independent secondary sources**, a stronger evidentiary basis than most figures in this assessment.

![Figure 2. Digitising Ebola-worker payments cut payment time by three-quarters](/home/user/felei/Research_Archive/WS1/docx/figures/s8_fig1_ebola_payment_time.png)

- **Persistence, despite a decade-old proof point:** broader PFM digitisation has moved slowly and partially even with this result in hand — IFMIS has taken more than a decade (since 2013) to reach 61 MDAs. This suggests the binding constraint is not proof-of-concept (which Sierra Leone itself has already generated) but sustained implementation capacity — consistent with, and sharpening, the existing draft's "talent is the binding constraint" framing (see Part 5).

## Part 3 — Is this sector investable now?

- **Confirmed: no venture-scale, Sierra Leonean-founded GovTech company was evidenced.** The existing draft's core finding stands.

- **Private vendors do participate as contractors — the existing draft names Monime (COVID-19 test-payment portal); this pass adds a second, more current, and structurally more significant example.** The Sierra Leone government signed an **MoU with Qhala, a Nairobi-based (Kenyan) firm**, to embed AI into the civil service — **500 civil servants to be trained**, with AI-driven workflows to be developed to improve public-service delivery. [via search] — **†, not opened**. **This is procurement revenue flowing to a foreign company, not a Sierra Leonean one** — a sharper, more current illustration of the existing draft's point that "private vendors participate as contractors" than the COVID-portal example alone provides, and direct evidence that even where GovTech contracting activity exists in Sierra Leone today, the domestic capacity gap means outside firms win the work.

- **Real, if small-scale, local innovation-pipeline activity exists.** DSTI co-organised the **Mining Community Innovation Challenge (MCIC)** with IFC, the World Bank, the National Minerals Agency and **Sensi Tech Hub** (a named Freetown innovation space) — a September 2022 hackathon involving **93 youth in 16 teams** from Freetown and 12 mining communities, designed to prototype data-driven ideas with a path to viable start-ups. More recently, **"2025 Sierra Leone Innovates"** runs a **Big 5 Innovation Investment Challenge** explicitly tied to the MTNDP's Big Five Game Changers, including a **"Transforming Public Service"** pillar. [IFCSIA](https://ifcsia.org/sierra-leone-hackathon-open-data-competition-mining-community-innovation-challenge/); [MoCTI](https://mocti.gov.sl/innovation-series/) — both **†, not opened**. **No evidence was found of any resulting revenue-generating company** — this remains grant/competition-stage activity, not proof of an investable pipeline, but it is a real, named upgrade from the existing draft's assumption of nothing at all happening at the entrepreneurial edge of this sector.

- **Minimum viable business model:** unchanged from the existing draft — procurement contracts, not investment rounds, are how capital flows in this sector. The Qhala example shows the contract-winner need not even be Sierra Leonean.

- **Venture-scale potential:** **no**, confirmed, and reinforced by the Ebola case itself: even the sector's single strongest success story was a donor/UN-coordinated emergency intervention that **repurposed an existing commercial FinTech rail (mobile money)** rather than building bespoke government technology or funding a dedicated GovTech company. The lesson is arguably that Sierra Leone's best GovTech outcome to date was, structurally, a FinTech-sector achievement, not a GovTech-sector one (see Part 8).

- **Exit environment:** **no reliable data identified** — confirmed. The Qhala/Monime contracting pattern does not constitute an equity-investable structure of any kind.

## Part 5 — Employment and job readiness

- **Skills concentrate in a small DSTI cadre — confirmed, and now given sharper texture.** The government's own AI-modernisation initiative required contracting a **foreign firm (Qhala)** specifically to train 500 civil servants — direct, current evidence that domestic AI/GovTech skills capacity is insufficient even for the government's own stated modernisation needs. The **October 2025 national AI Readiness Assessment** (MoCTI with World Bank support), which explicitly evaluates **"Capacity" (skills and talent)** as one of three pillars alongside "Compute" and "Context," is official government acknowledgment that this is a live, assessed gap rather than merely an external observation. ## Part 6 — Investment innovation and de-risking

- **Confirmed: "De-risking is grant-based (donor TA), not commercial"** — the existing draft's finding stands, now evidenced in more detail via the SLDTP ($50m World Bank IDA grant) and the historical Ebola-era digitisation effort (UN/Better Than Cash Alliance-coordinated).

- **The Ebola case is itself worth flagging as a genuine investment-innovation finding, not merely a historical footnote.** It demonstrates a repeatable, evidenced playbook — **use existing commercial mobile-money rails for emergency government payment digitisation** — that delivered a clearly measured US$10.7 million return. This is the single most concrete, quantified "return on digitisation" figure found anywhere across all eight sectors in this assessment, and it is a case study **Sierra Leone itself has already produced.** Recommend flagging this explicitly to the Advisory Group: it is a genuinely usable, home-grown precedent for any future public-payments digitisation business case, rather than an imported comparator.

- **What would need to be true for a commercial investor to enter:** the existing draft's finding stands unchanged — there is no venture path for a "sell SaaS to Sierra Leone's government" company in principle, since the customer base is inherently public-sector-only and procurement-driven.

## Part 7 — Enabling environment

- **Institutions and named projects, more fully mapped than in the existing draft:** DSTI (Office of the President) and MoCTI, with specific evidenced projects — tax portal, land registry portal, EPETS-1 expenditure-management digitisation, an integrated education-analytics platform in development, and the WanGov citizen-services platform.

- **PFM systems, confirmed and quantified:** IFMIS (61 of MDAs, up from a baseline of 12, since 2013), T24, ASYCUDA World (see Part 1 nuance vs. Sector 5), and ITAS; a **Treasury Single Account** reform is also underway to strengthen fiscal control.

- **AI-specific policy, new to this pass:** the October 2025 national AI Readiness Assessment (Compute/Capacity/Context) and the Qhala MoU (Part 3/5).

- **A genuine, dated governance-reform signal not in the existing draft:** Sierra Leone's **2024–2028 National Action Plan embeds open contracting** specifically to reduce corruption and improve public-service delivery, and Sierra Leone **presented this work at a 2026 African Open Governance Conference in Morocco.** [Pan African Visions] — **†, not opened**.

- **Donor/development programmes active:** World Bank (SLDTP's $50m grant; the separate PFM Improvement and Consolidation Project); UN/Better Than Cash Alliance/UNCDF (historical Ebola-era digitisation); IFC (Mining Community Innovation Challenge, 2022).

- **National policy:** the MTNDP's "vibrant e-government" target (existing draft) is now tied specifically to the **"Transforming Public Service"** pillar of the Big Five Game Changers, which directly anchors the 2025 Sierra Leone Innovates challenge — a more concrete policy linkage than the existing draft carries.

## Part 8 — Comparative evidence

- **The existing draft found no regional commercial GovTech comparator with venture returns. This pass adds three real, well-evidenced comparators — they reinforce, rather than overturn, the "not venture" verdict, but sharpen what "success" would actually require.**

  1. **Rwanda's Irembo** hosts more than **200 government services** on one platform and had saved **50 million work hours** in administrative time as of 2024 — the clear regional gold standard. More strikingly, **Rwandan-built GovTech capability is now an export product**: Rwandan engineers built **Telemo, Guinea's new national e-procurement system.** [via search] — **†, not opened**. This is the model Sierra Leone would need to mature toward — it is currently an *importer* of foreign GovTech capacity (Qhala), nowhere near becoming an exporter.
  2. **Ghana** operates **GHANEPS**, a mature e-procurement platform with more than **7,800 completed bids**, under a structured Public Procurement Act (2003) framework, and launched a **GovTech Challenge in May 2025** connecting the Ghana Revenue Authority with outside companies to solve specific tax-administration problems — a more systematic, mature version of exactly the foreign-vendor-contracting model Sierra Leone is only beginning with Qhala.
  3. **Namibia's Nam-X**, an e-government interoperability system explicitly modelled on **Estonia's X-Road**, shows that "import a proven foreign digital-government architecture" can be a deliberate national strategy rather than an ad hoc contract — a more structured version of what Sierra Leone's Qhala MoU represents in embryo.

- **What failed, and what worked — reframed with a specific, generalisable lesson.** No Sierra Leone-specific GovTech company failure was evidenced (there being essentially nothing at company scale to fail). The one clear, well-evidenced success (the 2014 Ebola mobile-money reform) worked precisely because it **repurposed an existing commercial FinTech rail** rather than building bespoke government technology from scratch. The generalisable lesson for Stage 2 and for WS3 (instrument fit) is that Sierra Leone's best public-sector digitisation outcome to date is, structurally, a **FinTech-sector achievement applied to a government use case** — not evidence that a dedicated GovTech company or investment vehicle is the right instrument here.

> **Verdict.** **Not investable as venture — confirmed**, and now with the richest evidentiary base of any "not investable" sector in this assessment, anchored by the single best-quantified digitisation-ROI case study found across all eight sectors (the 2014 Ebola mobile-money reform, US$10.7 million saved, corroborated across four independent sources). **What would have to change**, sharpened from the existing draft's general "shift to recurring paid SaaS-for-government contracts": Sierra Leone would need either **(a)** enough domestic technical capacity that Sierra Leonean firms, not Kenyan or other foreign contractors, win the government's own digitisation work — the Qhala example shows this gap is live and current, not merely historical — or **(b)** to mature toward the Rwanda model, where a domestically built platform becomes exportable regional infrastructure. Neither is remotely close on current evidence. The one genuinely actionable insight from this pass is that Sierra Leone's clearest public-sector digitisation success reused an *existing FinTech rail* rather than building new government-specific technology — any future public-sector digitisation thesis should test for that pattern specifically, rather than defaulting to a bespoke GovTech investment frame.
